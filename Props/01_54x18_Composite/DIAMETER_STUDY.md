# Diameter study: is a larger prop worth leaving the 54″ brief?

Generated by `make_diameter_report.py` from `results/diameter_study.json`. Same method as Phase 2 (XFOIL polars, BEMT, minimum-induced-loss
hover design with the manufacturing rules), repeated at 54″, 57″, 60″, 62″. Hot day, MTOW 280 lb, three rotors, two blades.
For each diameter the design-RPM sweep is run at the same tip speeds as the 54″ sweep, and the design with the best T/W at the sagged
pack voltage is picked for each motor. **No two-point refinement here**, so the 54″ column is the unrefined design and differs slightly
from the Phase 2 final blade (which gained about 2 % in energy from that step). Compare columns with each other, not with Phase 2.

## Answer

- **Energy: 60″ needs 13 % less battery energy than 54″** (7.62 → 6.65 kWh on the U15XL). Hover power per rotor drops from 5.65 to 5.01 kW.
- **Payload barely moves: 149 → 151 lb** (303 Wh/kg) or 120 → 126 lb (200 Wh/kg). The 3.2–4.8 kg of battery saved is
  mostly spent on heavier blades (+120 g each) and a longer-armed frame (+1.4 kg). Both of those masses are assumptions.
- **Thrust margin is the real gain.** On the U15XL, T/W at 84 V rises from 1.81 to 1.96, and on a fresh pack from 1.98 to 2.14.
  With the U15XXL the 60″ reaches **2.10 at 84 V**, so the T/W 2.0 target is met on a sagged pack from 60″ up (the U15XL needs 62″).
- **Motor heating eases but does not go away:** hover power is 1.76× the U15XL's rated power at 54″ and 1.56× at 60″.
- **Costs:** the rotor arm grows from 0.87 to 0.97 m (vehicle footprint about 11 % wider), blade root moments in cruise rise
  (84 ± 42 → 91 ± 44 N·m), and the flex, modal and balance targets get harder on a longer blade.

**Recommendation.** If the vehicle exists to carry payload 10 nmi, a 60″ prop buys little payload. If T/W 2.0 and motor temperature
matter (a tricopter has no motor-out case), 60″ is clearly better, and 60″ with the U15XXL is the only combination here that meets T/W 2.0
on a sagged pack below 62″. The energy gain is close to linear in diameter (57″ gives 53 % of the 54→60″ saving), so 57″ is a proportional middle step. This changes the program's baseline, so it is your call.

The KV29 motor turns slowly, so its best designs are high-pitch, chord-capped blades with a lower FoM, more torque and larger cyclic
loads. It pays off only where the extra current rating turns into thrust margin, i.e. at 60″ and above.

## What this rests on

1. Blade mass is a straight line through the vendor's 52″ (430 g) and 57″ (530 g) blades, extrapolated above 57″. For a 62″ blade the
   vendor sheet says 920 g and a reseller says 495 g, so the 60–62″ blade masses here could be well off. The real number comes from Phase 5.
2. Frame mass is 10 % of MTOW at 54″ and is scaled with arm length. No frame design exists.
3. Motor limits are the vendor's peak-current ratings with the Phase 1 efficiency and loaded-speed factors. No T/W here is thermally verified.
4. BEMT caveats from Phase 2 apply unchanged (clean-surface polars, no compressibility drag rise, rigid-blade forward flight, no
   rotor-to-rotor interference). The diameter trend rests on momentum theory (induced power ∝ 1/D) and is the most robust part.
5. Chord cap 0.22 R, design cl 1.0, two blades, as in Phase 2. Three blades were not studied.
